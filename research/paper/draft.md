# Retrieval Is Not Grounding: Stage-Wise Evaluation of an Evidence-Backed Exercise Recommender

*Draft — CSE498R*

---

## 1. Introduction

A growing class of applications retrieves peer-reviewed literature and presents it as
justification for a recommendation. Placing a citation next to advice is a strong claim: it
asserts that the recommendation follows from published science. In health-adjacent domains
that claim carries weight with users precisely because they cannot check it.

Such systems are conventionally evaluated **at the retriever** — precision, recall and nDCG
on the search step — on the implicit assumption that the retriever is the component that
determines whether output is well grounded. A better retriever, the reasoning goes, produces
better-supported citations.

We test that assumption on a deployed system and find that it does not hold. The system
studied is a retrieval-augmented exercise recommender that assembles a corpus of PubMed
studies, embeds it in a vector store, grades each study by design and recency, and attaches
citations to recommendations drawn from a catalog of 1,324 exercises. Measuring only its
retriever would have reported a working system. Measuring each stage separately shows that
the retriever is not the binding constraint, and that every substantive grounding failure
lives in the inexpensive logic surrounding it.

We make two contributions.

**A zero-annotation-cost evaluation protocol.** Literature-RAG pipelines already produce
their own ground truth, and it is usually discarded. The bibliographic index supplies
independent study-design labels. The boolean queries used to assemble the corpus supply
relevance labels, generated by a different retrieval paradigm than the dense search under
test. Three of the four stages we evaluate need no manual labelling at all; the fourth needs
a few dozen judgments.

**Evidence that stage-wise evaluation finds what retriever-only evaluation cannot.** We
report a case in which retrieval metrics are statistically flat across a design choice
(embedding model) that nonetheless changes the majority of the citations the user is shown,
and in which the dominant lever on retrieval quality is not the model at all but how the
query is written. We then act on that finding and re-measure: scoping the query to the
user's actual subject raises held-out retrieval quality by 137% (p = 0.0088) and improves
every embedding model tested, which turns the audit's largest observation into a validated
recommendation.

## 2. Related work

Retrieval evaluation rests on test collections of query–document relevance judgments, which
are expensive; that expense is why most deployed literature-RAG systems ship unevaluated.
Distant supervision — deriving labels from an artefact correlated with the target rather
than from annotators — is well established in relation extraction and in click-log-based IR.
Our provenance labels are an instance of it specific to corpus construction.

Recent RAG evaluation frameworks score faithfulness and answer relevance end to end, usually
with an LLM adjudicator. End-to-end scoring is complementary to what we do here: it reports
*that* grounding failed, whereas a stage-wise audit localises *where*, which is what a
maintainer needs in order to act. Our specificity-graded rubric is closer to the citation-
verification literature, which asks whether a cited source entails a specific claim rather
than whether it is merely on topic.

## 3. System under test

The system separates offline pipeline from request-time engine.

**Corpus.** 103 boolean PubMed queries, organised into 62 topic clusters, retrieve 502
studies (2018–2026), chunked into 1,264 passages. Each study is classified into an evidence
tier by an ordered keyword cascade over its MEDLINE publication types and its title and
abstract, scored `meta_analysis` 1.0 down to `other` 0.4. A retrieval weight combines tier,
recency and keyword relevance:

```
recency      = clamp(1 − (current_year − publication_year) / 10, 0, 1)
weight_doc   = 0.55·tier + 0.25·recency + 0.20·relevance
weight_chunk = clamp(0.85·weight_doc + 0.15·chunk_relevance, 0, 1)
```

**Retrieval.** Chunks are embedded with `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` into ChromaDB under cosine
distance. At request time one templated query is issued; results beyond a fixed ceiling of
0.50 are dropped, the remainder are re-ranked by a lexical keyword-overlap score, and an
acceptance gate discards anything below a lexical threshold.

**Attribution.** Independently of retrieval, each of the 1,324 catalogued exercises is
linked offline to studies whose text contains one of that exercise's name aliases. The
project documents this as movement-level rather than per-variant attribution, and states the
trade-off explicitly: a bench-press study is attributed to every bench-press variant.

**Generation.** A planner model chooses the weekly split. It is instructed never to emit a
PMID; the response schema has no citation field. Every citation is attached deterministically
by Python after validation. Hallucinated citations are therefore not possible, which is why
this paper asks whether attached citations *support* their claims rather than whether they
are fabricated.

## 4. Method

We decompose the path from bibliographic record to on-screen citation into four stages and
evaluate each against an independent reference.

| Stage | Mechanism | Reference | Annotation |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 study grading | keyword cascade | MEDLINE publication types | none |
| S2 retrieval | dense search + ceiling | corpus provenance (`topic_clusters`) | none |
| S3 attribution | alias string matching | human + model adjudication | 25 items |
| S4 presentation | score, citation, appendix | the system's published methodology | none |

**S1 reference.** Because the shipped classifier tests `pattern in publication_types or
pattern in title_and_abstract`, the reference condition is *the same function* restricted to
publication types. No mapping of ours mediates between the system and its ground truth, and
the comparison isolates one clause. We evaluate three nested views — publication types only,
plus title, plus abstract body — so the marginal cost of each widening is attributable.

**S2 labels.** A study is relevant to a query about cluster *C* iff *C*'s boolean PubMed
query fetched it. These labels are incomplete: a study fetched by one cluster may be relevant
to another and is scored as a miss. That biases precision downward only, so reported
precision is a lower bound; recall is defined as recall against the boolean-query result set.
The unit of analysis is the cluster (n = 49 present in the corpus), with bootstrap 95%
intervals and paired Wilcoxon tests across arms.

**S2 arms.** Five embedding models spanning general and biomedical pretraining. Critically,
`bge-base-en-v1.5` (general), `pubmedbert-base-embeddings` (biomedical) and
`S-PubMedBert-MS-MARCO` (biomedical, retrieval-trained) are all 109.5M parameters at 768
dimensions, so any domain effect is not a size effect. Four query formulations — the boolean
string, the bare topic, the topic as a question, and the production query template with
request fields held constant — isolate wording from model.

We use exact brute-force cosine rather than the production approximate index: it removes
approximate-search error as a confound, is bit-reproducible, and leaves the committed vector
store untouched.

**S2 two-stage measurement.** Stage 1 scores the retriever's own ranking. Stage 2 replays the
production post-processing — ceiling, lexical re-rank, acceptance gate — and compares the
resulting citation sets across arms by Jaccard overlap, both at the shipped fixed ceiling and
with every arm given the baseline's pool size, which separates threshold transfer from
genuine ranking difference.

**S3 rubric.** Each (exercise, study) pair is judged three times at increasing specificity:
did participants perform the movement; does the study report a relevant outcome; did it use
this exact variant. Both attachment mechanisms — alias match and the semantic fallback path —
are scored on the identical rubric so they can be compared rather than only critiqued.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 The study-design grader inflates, and can only inflate

Against MEDLINE tags the shipped grader agrees on 80.1% of 502 studies (κ = 0.719;
linear-weighted 0.765). All 100 disagreements grade the study *stronger*: 19.9% up, 0.0%
down (Figure 1).

This asymmetry is structural rather than empirical. The classifier is a first-match cascade
ordered strongest-tier-first, testing a union of text sources. Enlarging that union can only
introduce matches, and an introduced match can only fire at or before the position the
original match occupied. Reading more text can therefore only promote a study's tier.

Restricting the cascade to publication types plus **title** yields 90.8% agreement
(κ = 0.866); the abstract body alone accounts for a further 11.0 points of promotion. Mean
tier-score inflation is +0.033, propagating to +0.018 mean retrieval weight. The system
labels 60 studies `meta_analysis` where MEDLINE supports 37.

The error reaches the interface. Re-deriving exercise confidence with corrected tiers moves
117 of 346 evidenced exercises, changes the displayed confidence *level* for 19, and changes
which study is shown first for 38.

### 5.2 Query formulation dominates embedding choice

No embedding model differs significantly from the shipped one on the production formulation
(Wilcoxon p ≥ 0.062); the range across all five is 0.064 nDCG@10. The range across query
formulations is 0.400 — roughly six times larger (Figure 2). The shipped model scores 0.446
on the raw boolean query and 0.086 on the production template, losing 81%. The best model on
the production template scores below the worst model on the raw query.

Domain-specific pretraining did not help. At identical parameter count and dimensionality,
`bge-base` (general) reaches 0.444 on topic queries while `PubMedBERT` reaches 0.267 — the
worst arm tested. The biomedical model that performs competitively (0.418) is the one
additionally trained for retrieval. On this corpus the discriminating factor is retrieval
training, not biomedical domain.

### 5.3 Repairing the dominant lever

Because formulation dominates, we treated it as an intervention rather than an
observation. Five candidate rewrites of the query builder were evaluated against the same
provenance labels, with every query constructed as a live request constructs it: the
cluster's own muscle, real candidate shortlists from ``filter_and_score_exercises``, and
equipment present on half the requests so that variant differing only in equipment is
genuinely exercised.

To avoid selecting a winner on the queries used to report its gain, the 49 clusters were
split once, deterministically, by cluster id. The winner was chosen on the 25-query
selection half and its effect read off the 24-query held-out half.

The winning formulation carries only the target muscles, the goal, and the user's own
notes. On held-out queries it scores nDCG@10 0.215 against 0.091 for the field-list
formulation — a 137% relative gain, p = 0.0088, better on 12 of the 13 queries that moved.
Two component questions are answered along the way: equipment tokens are neutral
(0.215 either way), and candidate exercise names are actively harmful, because catalog
product names carry variant and equipment words that abstracts never use.

The gain is not model-specific. Applying the same formulation across all five embedding
arms improves every one, from +94% for the retrieval-trained biomedical model to +262% for
PubMedBERT (Figure 7). Nor is it bought by retrieving less: replaying the full production
post-processing, the share of requests yielding at least one citation is unchanged at 86%,
with mean citations per request 5.02 against 4.96.

### 5.4 Retrieval quality does not predict citation content

Although no arm is significantly better at retrieval, swapping the embedding model changes
most of the citations the pipeline emits: mean Jaccard overlap with the baseline citation set
is 0.32. Giving every arm the baseline's pool size raises overlap to 0.45, so approximately
half the instability is the fixed distance ceiling failing to transfer between embedding
spaces and half is genuine ranking difference. Retrieval metrics were flat across a change
that rewrote the bibliography.

### 5.5 The distance ceiling is a property of the embedding space

The shipped ceiling of 0.50 admits 4.3% of the corpus under MiniLM. Under `bge-small`,
`bge-base` and `S-PubMedBERT` it admits 94.6–100% and rejects none of our off-topic probes;
under `PubMedBERT` it admits 1.5% (Figure 3). The best separating ceiling ranges from 0.200
to 0.525. The system's only hard filter on semantic retrieval silently becomes a no-op if
the embedding model is changed.

The documented calibration also overstates the filter for the shipped model: in-domain
nearest-neighbour distances reach 0.821, beyond the claimed 0.23–0.45, so some legitimate
queries are silenced; and four of five adjacent off-topic probes — including the yoga,
nutrition and swimming cases the code comment names — pass the 0.50 filter.

### 5.6 The corpus holds the science; name matching loses it

Only 346 of 1,324 exercises (26.1%) carry any citation and only 145 of 502 studies (28.9%)
ever attach to one; at request time coverage falls to 18.9% for hypertrophy. Coverage ranges
from 0% to 64% across muscles and does not follow corpus supply (Figure 4). The pipeline ran
dedicated gap-filling queries and fetched 20 grip-and-forearm studies; none of the 37 forearm
exercises cites any of them. The same holds for traps (0/17) and hip abductors (0/5, from 30
fetched studies).

The mechanism explains the pattern. Of 1,970 distinct catalog aliases only 57 ever match a
study (2.9%), and 49 of those are exactly two words long; matched aliases average 2.21 words
against 3.59 for the catalog as a whole. Attribution fires only for movements that happen to
have a short, generic name. Consequently one study is cited for up to 47 exercises (mean
13.2), and the 346 evidenced exercises share only 50 distinct citation lists, the largest
group comprising 36 exercises with byte-identical citations.

The request-time goal gate narrows little: 88.3% of surviving hypertrophy links and 95.2% of
strength links pass it via an inferred-modality shortcut whose pattern list contains the bare
token "strength".

### 5.7 Support collapses as claims become specific

On an adjudicated pilot (n = 22 alias-matched pairs), 59.1% of citations involve the
movement, 31.8% report a relevant outcome, 13.6% used the actual variant, and 9.1% satisfy
all three (Figure 5). The failure taxonomy is led by movement-but-not-variant support
(31.8%), followed by different population or modality (18.2%), unrelated topic (18.2%), and —
a category we did not anticipate — the matched term naming a **clinical assessment test
rather than an exercise** (13.6%). The alias "leg raise" matched a randomised trial of
deep-oscillation massage therapy for hamstring flexibility.

### 5.8 Conformance, and what the system gets right

Of eleven published claims audited, six conformed, four diverged and one was qualified.
All four divergences were remediable and were remediated (§7); the conformance module
derives each published figure from its source file rather than a snapshot, so re-running
it now reports the corrected state and serves as a regression check. The most consequential
guardrail **held** unchanged throughout: the heuristic confidence path is capped at 0.72, below the
0.75 "high" threshold, and no zero-evidence exercise is labelled "high" (maximum 0.64).
However, "moderate" is applied to 1,006 exercises of which only 5.9% have any linked study
(Figure 6). Separately, prescription values are goal-dependent constants — verified by
calling the engine's own seed function on dissimilar exercises and obtaining identical output
— so a citation displayed beside a set-and-rep prescription supports the exercise choice and
never the dose.

Three design decisions verified as sound: the ranking surfaces evidenced exercises 2.8× more
often than the remainder of the catalog; retrieval returns an honestly empty result rather
than padding with weak matches; and ranking ties are surfaced rather than concealed.

## 6. Remediation

Four of the audit's findings were acted on, chosen for being minimal, verifiable, and
covered by the existing test suite. All 73 engine tests pass unchanged.

| Change | Finding addressed | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Query builder scoped to muscles, goal and user notes | §5.2, §5.3 | +137% nDCG@10 held-out |
| Citation-containment check runs on every generation | §5.8 | guardrail moves from test-only to executed |
| API appendix merges per-exercise references | §5.8 | study count covers every displayed PMID |
| Two published counts corrected | §5.8 | conformance divergences resolved |

One finding was deliberately **not** acted on. Restricting the study-design grader to
publication types and titles would remove more than half the tier inflation, and §5.1
already measures that counterfactual. Making it take effect, however, requires regrading
the committed corpus and then rebuilding both the exercise catalog and the vector store,
since tier scores are baked into all three artefacts; that would shift catalog rankings
which the existing quality tests assert on. We report the recommendation and its measured
effect rather than taking that risk, which is the conservative choice for a change whose
benefit is already quantified.

## 7. Discussion

The system's engineering attention went to the retriever, and the retriever is the one stage
that is broadly fine. What determines the trustworthiness of a citation here is the grading
heuristic upstream of it, the query template feeding it, the threshold truncating it, and the
string matcher downstream that decides which recommendation the citation lands on.

The mechanisms generalise beyond this application. **First-match keyword cascades over
enlarged text inflate monotonically** — any system grading documents this way has a
one-directional bias, provable from the control flow. **Absolute distance thresholds do not
transfer across embedding spaces**, so a threshold and an embedding model are a coupled pair
that must be recalibrated together. **String-match attribution is biased toward entities with
short generic names**, producing coverage that is uncorrelated with corpus supply and
collapsing distinct entities onto identical evidence. **Blending heuristic priors with
evidence into one displayed score** yields a score that ranks correctly but cannot express
the absence of evidence.

Each has a concrete remedy: restrict the cascade to indexed metadata and titles; recalibrate
the ceiling per embedding space, or use rank cut-offs; replace or supplement alias matching
with retrieval-based attribution; and separate "how much evidence" from "how confident" in
the interface.

## 8. Limitations

The evaluation covers one system and one corpus, so the magnitudes reported are not
themselves general — the mechanisms are what we claim transfer. Provenance labels are
incomplete, and we handle that by reporting precision as a lower bound and by resting
conclusions on relative comparisons where the label bias is shared. MEDLINE indexing is
itself imperfect, which is why we separate disagreement from error via human adjudication of
a stratified disagreement sample rather than treating the index as truth. The adjudicated
citation sample is a pilot bounded by API quota; the deterministic findings do not depend on
it. The production-template arm holds request fields constant to isolate the template, so it
measures template overhead rather than end-to-end production retrieval quality; the
intervention in §5.3 is measured separately under realistic request construction. That
intervention is validated on held-out queries drawn from the same 49 clusters and the same
corpus, so it establishes that the formulation was the binding constraint here, not that
the specific winning formulation is optimal in general. Its effect is measured on retrieval
quality; we did not measure whether better-retrieved findings change what a reader
concludes.

## 9. Conclusion

Evaluating a literature-grounded recommender at its retriever measures the stage least likely
to be the problem. We presented a stage-wise audit that localises grounding failure, showed
that three of its four stages require no manual annotation because such pipelines already
store their own ground truth, and applied it to a deployed system. Retrieval was adequate and
statistically indistinguishable across five embedding models; the failures were a
monotonically inflating grading heuristic, a query template costing six times more than model
choice, a distance threshold valid for exactly one embedding space, and a name-matching
attribution step that leaves three-quarters of the catalog uncited while the relevant science
sits in the corpus. Acting on the largest of those findings — scoping the query to the
user's actual subject — raised held-out retrieval quality by 137% across every embedding
model tested, without retrieving less, which confirms that the formulation rather than the
retriever was the binding constraint. Retrieval is not grounding, and measuring the first
does not tell you about the second.

## 10. Reproducibility

The harness runs against committed data with no network access for the deterministic stages.
S1, S3 and S4 reproduce byte-identically across runs; S2 does so given the same models and
seed. Every figure derives from a JSON result file, each result file records the package
versions and seed that produced it, and no module writes outside `research/`. Sampling seeds
are pinned and reported.
